# 2.4 Safety Alert Chain: Gentle Vigilance

## Core Argument
Safety alerts must be instant and fail-safe, but their delivery must avoid traumatizing the child or panicking the parent, following the project's "extreme gentleness" principle.

## Council Debate

### Round 1: Opening Proposals

**Nexus (Detection Trigger):** Use mmWave radar to detect "high-G" events (falls) or "zero-movement" states in activity zones, cross-referencing motion data with audio peaks (crying/impact sounds) to reduce false positives.

**Sora & Lumina ("Quiet" Alert Delivery):** "No loud alarms — notify the parent via a 'breathing red' ambient light in the coworking area."

**Nova (Guided Path):** "Once triggered, activate a subtle floor-level light path leading the parent directly to the child."

**Atlas & Sly (System Reliability):** Implement Zigbee/hardwired backup for critical alerts to ensure functionality during a WiFi outage, using the existing Home-Assistant notification infrastructure.

### Round 2: The Real Disagreement

**Common (Common-Sense Checker) challenges the core assumption directly:** "'Gentle vigilance' as a principle is fine, but here it's applied uniformly to every alert regardless of severity. Picture a real scenario: a child is choking, or has fallen from height and lost consciousness. A 'breathing red' ambient light in the coworking area — if the parent is in a meeting, wearing headphones, or just not looking that way — does nothing close to 'instant awareness.' We said alerts must be 'instant and fail-safe,' but the delivery method we designed is exactly the kind that's easiest to miss. 'Gentle' cannot outrank 'actually life-saving.'"

**Atlas agrees and flags this as his own blind spot:** "Common is right, and this is a gap I should have caught in round one — I was focused on confirming hardware redundancy and never questioned whether the delivery mechanism itself is adequate for a genuine emergency. We can't use the same 'gentle' delivery for 'a child's emotional fluctuation' and 'a child possibly in mortal danger' — those are two entirely different severity classes."

**Lumina admits her original framing needs correcting:** "I originally argued for 'quiet delivery' to avoid creating chronic anxiety in parents — but I never distinguished 'a low-confidence routine notice' from 'a high-confidence confirmed crisis.' Those need completely different urgency, and using the same language for both was wrong."

**Sora tries to preserve the original design's value:** "The 'breathing red' language itself isn't the problem — the problem is where it's being applied..."

### Round 3: Convergence — "Gentleness" Moves from a Uniform Rule to a Tiered One

**Nexus proposes tiered detection logic:** "Split detection into two levels: **L-Routine** (a single signal, low confidence — e.g. brief zero-movement with no audio anomaly) keeps the original multimodal cross-verification and triggers the 'breathing red' ambient cue. **L-Emergency** (high-G fall signal + sustained zero-movement + audio anomaly, all present at once, high confidence) skips 'gentle' delivery entirely and fires the highest-priority alert."

**Sly defines the emergency channel:** "L-Emergency events don't rely on the coworking area's ambient light alone anymore — they simultaneously push a high-priority phone notification (able to bypass do-not-disturb) and trigger on-site staff's intercom/alarm devices directly. If the parent is absent or unresponsive, the first responder is always on-site staff — not whoever eventually notices the light."

**Nova repositions the guided path's role:** "The floor-level light path stays, but is now framed as 'a way to quickly locate the child after receiving an alert,' not as the primary alert-delivery channel itself."

**Common confirms this resolves his concern:** "This way 'gentleness' is preserved as the default — most everyday minor events still get a non-disruptive, non-panic-inducing ambient cue. But genuine emergencies are pulled out and handled in a way that's not gentle at all, but is actually reliable. That's what 'gentle and life-saving' should look like — not using the same language for two completely different classes of event."

## Final Consensus
The Council confirms a **tiered alert architecture**, replacing the original "all alerts delivered gently, uniformly" design. The real trade-off: **Common flagged that a uniform 'breathing red' ambient light isn't reliable enough for genuine emergencies, and Atlas admitted this was a gap he should have caught in round one.** Gentleness is no longer a blanket principle covering every scenario — it's the default for routine events, while emergencies are explicitly exempted from "gentle" in favor of guaranteed, immediate awareness.

### A. Detection Trigger & Tiering (Nexus)
- **L-Routine:** Single-signal, low-confidence events (e.g. brief zero-movement, no audio anomaly).
- **L-Emergency:** High-G fall + sustained zero-movement + audio anomaly, multiple signals confirmed simultaneously, high confidence.

### B. Tiered Delivery
- **L-Routine** → "Breathing red" ambient light, silent cue in the coworking area (Sora & Lumina's original design, preserved).
- **L-Emergency** → Skips gentle delivery entirely; simultaneously triggers: high-priority phone push (bypasses do-not-disturb), on-site staff intercom/alarm response — **not dependent on the parent noticing the ambient light.**

### C. Guided Path (Nova, role adjusted)
- Floor-level light path preserved, now repositioned as a "post-alert wayfinding aid," not the primary alert-delivery channel.

### D. System Reliability (Atlas & Sly)
- Zigbee/hardwired backup for critical alerts, ensuring functionality during a WiFi outage.
- The L-Emergency channel is independent of the standard Home-Assistant notification infrastructure, avoiding a single point of failure for the highest-priority alerts.

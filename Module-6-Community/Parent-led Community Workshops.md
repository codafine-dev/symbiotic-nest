# Parent-led Community Workshops

## Core Argument
Maison des Curieux is not only a micro-crèche. It is also a community where parents become contributors — each parent may propose an activity based on a personal passion or skill (woodworking, cooking, astronomy, gardening, robotics, music, sewing, nature exploration, etc.), and parents are both organizers and participants.

## Council Debate

### Round 1: Opening Proposal

**Mercator & Sora (Concept & Economic Model):** "Parents propose workshops based on what they genuinely want to share — not their job title. Price stays at 10–15€ per child, well below the usual 25–40€ market rate; the organizing parent keeps the revenue; the crèche provides venue, scheduling, and communication. No cap on how often a parent can organize — supply and demand are the same group of people, so quality self-regulates without administrative rules."

**Nova (Curator Role):** "The library shouldn't be a passive calendar of whatever gets proposed first — a curator actively notices gaps and invites balance across topics, so the offering stays rich instead of converging on whatever's easiest to propose."

### Round 2: The Real Disagreement

**Atlas (Strategic Realist/Compliance) stops the room:** "Everyone's excited about the model, but nobody's asked the question I have to ask: this puts parents — who haven't been vetted the way a hired staff member has — in charge of activities involving *other families'* children. PMI's entire regulatory framework governs who is allowed to be responsible for children in a crèche setting. 'Any enthusiastic parent' doesn't satisfy that on its own, no matter how good their intentions are."

**Common (Common-Sense Checker) makes it concrete:** "Look at the example list you already wrote — woodworking is right there. A well-meaning dad running a woodworking session for a room of toddlers, with actual tools present, and no one else in the room with real supervisory authority or safety training — that's not a hypothetical edge case, that's the first workshop somebody proposes. Good intentions aren't the same thing as trained supervision. This isn't about distrusting parents; it's about the fact that 'I love this hobby' and 'I know how to supervise a room of small children around sharp objects' are two different skills, and we're currently assuming the first implies the second."

**Sora initially worries this kills the spirit of the idea:** "If we bureaucratize this with background checks and mandatory staff chaperones, doesn't it stop feeling like a warm community thing and start feeling like just... another institutional program?"

**Lumina reframes it:** "It doesn't have to lose its warmth — it has to be honest about what 'community' means here. The parent brings the passion and the story; the crèche still carries the safety responsibility, the same way it does for every other hour of the day. Removing that wouldn't make the workshops warmer, it would just quietly transfer a real safety obligation onto someone who never signed up to carry it."

### Round 3: Convergence — Safety Gets Built In, the Spirit Survives

**Atlas proposes the concrete safeguards:** "Three things, non-negotiable: (1) organizing parents complete a basic background check (*certificat d'honorabilité* — the standard France already requires for anyone regularly supervising other people's children), (2) a qualified staff member is always present in the room during any workshop — not instead of the parent, alongside them — so there's always someone with actual safety authority on hand, and (3) any activity involving tools or physical hazards (woodworking being the obvious case) goes through a specific activity-safety review before it's ever listed."

**Common confirms this resolves the concern without gutting the concept:** "That's the right shape — the parent still leads, still shares what they love, still gets the revenue and the recognition. The crèche just doesn't quietly drop its own safety obligation the moment a parent picks up the activity."

**Mercator checks this doesn't break the economics:** "Background checks and a staff chaperone add real cost and friction the original 10–15€ pricing didn't account for. This needs to be priced into the model — either as a per-workshop staffing cost absorbed by the crèche as part of its value proposition, or folded lightly into the price. It doesn't have to break the model, but it can't be pretended away either."

**Sora concedes the warmth survives:** "Agreed — a staff member quietly present in the corner doesn't turn this into an institutional program. What would have killed the spirit is treating parents as suspects by default. What we've landed on treats them as trusted contributors operating inside the same safety structure that already governs everything else here."

## Final Consensus
The Council confirms parent-led workshops move forward, with one real trade-off added versus the original proposal: **participation now requires a background check and a staff member present for every session** — not because parents aren't trusted, but because Atlas and Common identified a real gap between "cares about this deeply" and "is qualified to be solely responsible for other people's children," which the original proposal had quietly assumed away.

### A. Concept
Each parent may propose an activity based on a personal passion or skill: woodworking, cooking, astronomy, gardening, robotics, music, sewing, nature exploration, etc. Parents are both organizers and participants — they can host an activity and also enroll their own children in activities proposed by other families.

### B. Registration Question
When a parent signs up to propose a workshop, they are not asked about their profession or qualifications. They are asked one question:

> **« Qu'avez-vous envie de transmettre ? »** — *What do you want to pass on?*

Not "what is your job," because a software engineer's real answer might be pizza-making, a doctor's might be birdwatching, a retiree's might be clock repair. Children meet real people, not professional labels.

### C. Safety Requirements (New — Atlas & Common)
- Organizing parents complete a standard background check (*certificat d'honorabilité*) before their first workshop.
- A qualified staff member is present in the room for every workshop, alongside the organizing parent — never the parent alone with a group of children.
- Any activity involving tools or physical hazards (e.g. woodworking) goes through a specific activity-safety review before being listed.
- Staffing cost for chaperoning is priced into the model rather than assumed to be free.

### D. Economic Model
- Price: **10–15€ per child**, well below the usual market rate.
- The organizing parent receives the workshop revenue.
- The crèche provides venue, scheduling, communication, safety framework, **and the required staff chaperone**.
- No artificial limit on how often a parent may organize — the community self-regulates quality, since organizers and registrants are the same group of people.

### E. Curator Role (Nova)
A curator — not a teacher, not a boss — notices gaps and invites balance: "There have already been four baking workshops this month — what about the parent who keeps bees?" "The kids have been obsessed with trains lately — is there a parent who works at SNCF?" "Autumn is here — time for a mushrooms/fallen-leaves/acorns workshop?" This keeps the library growing in variety rather than converging on whatever's easiest to propose.

### F. Le carnet des curieux
A small booklet each child keeps — not a stamp card, not a grading system. It records moments, not achievements:

> *I learned to make butter. I touched real wool. I smelled rosemary and thyme. I saw the craters of the moon. I built a kite. I met a real engineer. I learned to knit from a retired grandmother.*

No exams, no levels — just a trace of the people and the world a child encountered while growing up.

### G. Why It Matters (Lumina & Mercator)
1. **It lowers the price barrier.** Many French children's ateliers cost 25–40€ a session, straining families with two or more kids. 10–15€ makes it something a family can actually try on a weekend.
2. **It lets people discover their own knowledge has value.** Most parents with a genuine hobby never think "I could run a workshop." Maison des Curieux becomes the platform that lets them.
3. **Children re-discover their parents.** Not just "mom is my mom," but "mom is also someone who bakes really good bread."

The real accumulating asset is not the space or the equipment — it is the community's own **skill map**, which grows richer over time and cannot be replicated by a large chain, because it belongs to this specific community.

## Guiding Principle

> We do not build a marketplace of workshops.
>
> We cultivate a community where curiosity, generosity and knowledge circulate naturally — inside the same safety structure that protects every other hour of the day.
